\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec::contrib1::literature}

\subsection{Robot behaviour and skill representation}
\label{sec::contrib1::literature::behaviourskill}

Section \ref{sec::LiteratureReview::RobotControlBBM} outlined behaviour-based systems as an approach for constructing complex behaviours by integrating more straightforward, reactive behaviours. These behaviours operate by locally processing sensory inputs to generate corresponding actions. Early behaviour-based models, such as subsumption architecture and motor schemas, primarily focused on immediate, sensor-driven responses rather than explicit, long-term task planning. However, their core principles of modularity and reactivity have significantly influenced the development of more structured architectures, such as Behaviour Trees (BTs) and Finite State Machines (FSMs), which are now widely used for task generation and management.

Modern behaviour-based systems often represent robot behaviours and skills as modular units that can be combined to execute complex tasks. These representations are frequently organised using symbolic approaches, where knowledge is encoded through predefined symbols and rules. While this enables precise and interpretable task execution, it can also limit adaptability in dynamic environments.

Behaviour Trees have become a popular tool for modeling robot behaviours and are increasingly used for task generation due to their inherent properties \cite{cao_robot_nodate, colledanchise_implementation_2021}. Task generation in this context refers to designing the high-level sequence of actions and conditions required to achieve a specific goal. BTs excel in modularity, where complex tasks can be constructed by composing simpler, reusable subtrees. Individual nodes and subtrees encapsulate specific behaviours or sub-tasks and can be easily reused in different contexts without affecting the overall structure. This facilitates the creation of complex tasks from well-defined, tested components \cite{biggar_principled_2021}. Recent research explores the automatic generation of behaviour trees (BTs) for robot tasks using methods such as Genetic Programming, Learning from Demonstration, autonomous planners, and large language models (LLMs) \cite{styrud_automatic_2024, izzo_btgenbot_2024, cao_robot_nodate}. LLMs, in particular, show promise in enabling cross-domain task generation without relying on pre-defined primitive tasks. Techniques like the Phase-Step prompt are designed to generate hierarchical BT structures from abstract task descriptions \cite{cao_robot_nodate}.

BTs are increasingly integrated into popular robotics frameworks, such as ROS (Robot Operating System), facilitating their adoption in real-world applications. Frameworks like ReBeT \cite{alberts_rebet_2024}extend BTs for architecture-based self-adaptation by incorporating quality requirements. PlanSys2 \cite{martin_plansys2_2021} also supports the efficient execution of plans as Behaviour Trees.

While modularity helps, very complex tasks can still result in significant and intricate behaviour trees, which may become challenging to manage if not structured carefully. \cite{biggar_principled_2021} points out the risk of "monolithic trees" that are hard to extend or refactor. Additionally, the Parallel node, while powerful for encoding concurrent behaviours, can introduce concurrency problems that require careful design and synchronisation mechanisms \cite{colledanchise_analysis_2019}. Basic BTs may be less expressive than FSMs in directly encoding memory and past decision history, although mechanisms like blackboard systems \cite{alberts_rebet_2024} can mitigate this limitation. In addition to that, traditional BTs might exhibit static behaviour prioritisation, where the order of child evaluation in control nodes determines priority, potentially leading to issues if a lower-priority behaviour should pre-empt a currently running higher-priority one due to environmental changes \cite{anguelov_behavior_nodate}. Ultimately, the effectiveness of task generation with BTs still relies on a well-defined set of primitive actions or skills that the leaf nodes can execute. While LLMs can help generate higher-level task structures, grounding them in actual robot capabilities remains a critical step \cite{cao_robot_nodate}.

\subsection{Using Behaviour Trees with LLMs}
\label{sec::contrib1::literature::behaviourllm}

LLMs can be integrated with behaviour-based control, particularly with BTs, to achieve automated task planning and dynamic replanning capabilities. In the literature, Methods like BETR-XP-LLM (Behavior Tree Expansion with Large Language Models) dynamically and automatically expand and configure BTs as policies for robot control \cite{styrud_automatic_2024}. This approach uses an LLM to resolve errors outside the task planner's capabilities, both during planning and execution. It can permanently update the policy to handle similar problems in the future. LLMs can also be used for high-level planning, generating sequences of sub-goals that are then implemented using a library of predefined robot skills within a BT structure \cite{liang_code_2023}.

In approaches like BOSS (Bootstrap Your Own Skills), LLMs guide the selection of skills from a library to chain them into new long-horizon behaviours. The resulting skill chains can be seen as forming or expanding a behaviour-based system structure \cite{zhang_bootstrap_2023}. 

LLMs can design reward functions from language instructions, which can then be used to learn the parameters of skills potentially organised within a behaviour-based system architecture \cite{kawaharazuka_real-world_2024}.

LLMs bring vast "world knowledge" and reasoning capabilities, allowing behaviour-based systems to handle more complex and unpredictable environments compared to an end-to-end learning based system that solely relies on the dataset \cite{hazra_saycanpay_2024}. In addition to "world knowledge" and reasoning, LLMs encompass human common sense, which can enable robots controlled by LLM-guided behavior-based systems to perform tasks such as motion planning and recognition more autonomously \cite{kawaharazuka_real-world_2024}. 

The ability of LLMs to understand and respond to natural language instructions enables more flexible task specification and adaptation of robot behaviour \cite{mahadevan_generative_2024}. Users can also interact with robots more intuitively \cite{lynch_interactive_2022}. LLMs can interpret high-level textual instructions and break them into sequences of executable skills within a BT \cite{ahn_as_2022}. Additionally, with LLM's zero-shot reasoning capabilities, behaviour-based systems can potentially generalise to new tasks and environments without extensive retraining \cite{liang_code_2023}.

Using LLMs for BT generation, if not managed carefully, could lead to "monolithic trees" that are difficult to extend or refactor, as highlighted by \cite{anguelov_behavior_nodate}. The complexity introduced by LLM-generated behaviours needs to be managed to maintain transparency and reusability.

A significant challenge is ensuring that the high-level plans generated by LLMs can be accurately translated into low-level robot actions within the behaviour-based system \cite{ahn_as_2022}. LLMs may produce plans not directly executable by the robot's available skills. In addition to non-compatible BTs, LLMs can sometimes produce incorrect or nonsensical outputs, known as hallucinations, which could lead to flawed behaviour-based plans or actions \cite{kawaharazuka_real-world_2024}. 

While powerful, LLMs may still struggle with the nuances of context, culture, emotion, and personal idiosyncrasies, which are essential for robust human-robot interaction within a behaviour-based framework \cite{zhang_large_2023}. Furthermore, according to \cite{zhang_large_2023}, as LLMs become increasingly integrated into robot control, concerns about safety and ethical implications in human-robot interaction emerge, particularly regarding biased or inappropriate outputs.

Many approaches require a well-defined library of robot skills or primitives for the LLM to utilise within the behaviour-based system. The quality and coverage of this skill library are crucial for the LLM to accurately understand the skills and effectively connect them \cite{vemprala_chatgpt_2023}. Beyond that, LLM-generated execution plans may not always account for the geometric and physical constraints of the environment and the robot, necessitating additional mechanisms in the skill library to encapsulate physical and geometric constraints \cite{lin_text2motion_2023}.

Ultimately, evaluating the performance of LLM-integrated behavior-based systems can be challenging due to the lack of standardised metrics and benchmarks \cite{firoozi_foundation_2023}.

In summary, combining behaviour-based systems with LLMs offers significant potential for creating more flexible, intelligent, and user-friendly robots. However, it also presents challenges related to grounding, reliability, complexity, and the need for careful system design and evaluation. The literature suggests that ongoing research is actively exploring methods to leverage the strengths of both approaches while mitigating their weaknesses.
