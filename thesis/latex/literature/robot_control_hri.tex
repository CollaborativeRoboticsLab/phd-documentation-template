\section{Robotics Systems for Human Robot Interaction}
\label{sec::LiteratureReview::RobotControlHRI}

Recent advancements in Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL) models have enabled their use as part of a robot's planning and control system. The primary focus connecting robots with these models has been to utilise them either as the core system of the robot \cite{brohan_rt-1_2023, brohan_rt-2_2023} or as part of the decision-making system of the robot, which focuses on generating execution plans \cite{Yuan_2024, macdonald_language_2024}. 

The ability to incorporate human interaction into learning-based systems \cite{brohan_rt-1_2023, brohan_rt-2_2023, Yuan_2024, macdonald_language_2024} enables versatile and dynamic human robot interactions. However, one drawback of this approach is the requirement for large datasets that encompass all possible human-robot interactions, as the performance of the learning-based system depends on the quality and completeness of the dataset. 

As an alternative, using LLMs has become popular in recent years, where the LLM generates an execution plan, and a behaviour-based system executes the plan \cite{cao_robot_nodate}. This has become feasible compared to building complex datasets for various interactions and robot models, as it separates the planning and control of the robot from the actual plan generation.

The following sections will discuss literature on the use of behaviour-based systems, learning-based systems for robots, LLM-based generative behaviour systems, VLM-based generative behaviour systems, VLA-based systems, and VLN-based systems.

\subsection{Behaviour based Robotic Systems}
\label{sec::LiteratureReview::RobotControlBBM}

Behaviour-based robotics decomposes complex robot behaviours into simpler, concurrently running modules known as behaviours or skills. These behaviours locally process sensory inputs and respond actions. Two basic behaviour-based control architectures are the subsumption architecture and motor schemas.

Subsumption Architecture consists of multiple layers of task-achieving behaviours, each capable of perceiving sensory information relevant to its specific task (e.g., obstacle avoidance, target seeking). Behaviours are selected competitively, increasing responsiveness and reducing planning complexity in dynamic and unknown environments \cite{nakhaeinia_review_2011}.

According to \cite{nakhaeinia_review_2011}, the Motor Schemas approach utilises the cooperative coordination of multiple behaviours. It allows for the simultaneous use of outputs from several behaviours, capturing their influence on the overall action.

Behaviour-based control supports incremental development of the navigation system layer by layer \cite{nakhaeinia_review_2011}. Additionally, the architecture provides increased robustness and reliability; if one behaviour unit fails, others can continue their tasks, leading to a navigation system with increased responsiveness. However, implementing coordination among behaviours can be challenging \cite{nakhaeinia_review_2011} because behaviours are typically low-level and do not directly reflect high-level tasks.

In addition to subsumption architecture and motor schemas, Behaviour Trees (BTs) and Hierarchical Finite State Machines (HFSMs) are also two of the behaviour-based systems that can be used, especially in the context of designing complex robot behaviours \cite{iovino_comparison_2024, cao_robot_nodate}.

Behaviour trees are a graphical, hierarchical representation for task-switching controllers that have gained significant popularity in robotics, originating from the computer games industry \cite{iovino_comparison_2024}. According to \cite{styrud_automatic_2024}, A BT is structured as a directed rooted tree. It consists of a single root node, internal nodes known as control flow nodes, and leaf nodes referred to as execution nodes, which can be either Action or Condition nodes. The execution of a BT begins at the root, which periodically sends an activation signal, called a tick, down the tree. Each node that receives a tick executes its function and returns a status to its parent: Success, Failure, or Running. The control flow nodes dictate how the tick signal is propagated to their children. Common control flow nodes include Sequence (ticks children sequentially until one fails or all succeed), Fallback (or Selector) (ticks children sequentially until one succeeds or all fail), Parallel (can execute multiple children concurrently according to a defined policy), and Decorator (modifies the behaviour of a single child).

BTs are inherently reactive, meaning they can respond immediately to environmental changes. The entire tree is evaluated at every tick, allowing for quick adaptation and the preemption of running actions if a higher-priority behaviour needs to execute \cite{iovino_comparison_2024}. Additionally, BTs are designed to be modular, where every subtree is a BT with cohesive behaviour. This allows for easy reuse of subtrees in different parts of the behaviour or other behaviours without compromising the overall structure \cite{iovino_comparison_2024, ghzouli2023behavior}. Each building block is also independent, facilitating separate testing. BTs offer flexibility in structuring complex behaviours through the combination of different control flow nodes and the ability to create custom nodes. They explicitly support task hierarchy and action sequencing \cite{ghzouli2023behavior}.

While the Parallel node enables concurrent execution, it can also introduce concurrency issues if not managed carefully \cite{colledanchise_analysis_2019}. Some research suggests that basic BTs might be less expressive than HFSMs because they may not inherently build behaviours based on internal variables and past decisions, as HFSMs can encode state history. However, extensions like memory nodes exist \cite{colledanchise2021implementation}.

A Finite State Machine is a control architecture represented as a labelled directed graph. The core concept is that the system exists in exactly one state at any given time. Transitions between these states occur in response to specific events or conditions. According to \cite{biggar_principled_2021}, an FSM comprises a set of states and transitions that link these states. The system starts in an initial state. When a defined event occurs and the conditions for a transition are met, the FSM moves from its current state to the next state. Actions can be associated with either the states (in Moore machines, the action is performed upon entering or being in the state) or the transitions (in Mealy machines, the action is performed during the transition). Hierarchical State Machines (HFSMs) or state charts extend the basic FSM model by allowing states to contain nested sub-states, which helps in managing complexity and increasing modularity \cite{ghzouli2023behavior, iovino_comparison_2024}.

FSMs offer an intuitive and straightforward design, making them easy to understand and implement across various applications and have been the state-of-the-art policy representation in robotics for many years, leading to a more mature understanding and more readily available tools and techniques \cite{iovino_comparison_2024}. Additionally, FSMs have a strong theoretical foundation, which allows for formal analysis, verification, and model checking \cite{ghzouli2023behavior}

However, as the complexity of the robot's behaviour increases, the number of states and transitions in an FSM can grow exponentially, making them fragile and challenging to manage, maintain, and debug. This is sometimes compared to the difficulties associated with unstructured programming, which often involves 'go to' statements \cite{biggar_principled_2021}. Additionally, FSMs can be less modular than BTs because state transitions are usually specific to the states involved, making it harder to reuse parts of the behaviour in different contexts without significant modifications \cite{cao_robot_nodate}. FSMs could also suffer from lower reactivity than BTs because transitions between discrete states determine the control flow, and responding to rapidly changing environments might require a large number of complex state transitions \cite{iovino_comparison_2024}. However, HFSMs can improve reactivity to some extent.

BTs and FSMs are high-level planning and control architectures for robots, coordinating lower-level skills or behaviours. BTs are often presented as a more modular and reactive alternative to FSMs, especially for complex tasks where maintainability and responsiveness are crucial \cite{iovino_comparison_2024}. Some work even shows how BTs can modularise HFSMs and that missions specified using one can be mapped to the other.

While the original behaviour-based methods, such as the subsumption architecture, focused on the competitive or cooperative blending of low-level behaviours without a central state representation, BTs and FSMs provide a more structured way to orchestrate these behaviours or higher-level actions. BTs achieve reactivity through their continuous ticking mechanism, which can trigger different branches of the tree based on the current sensory input and the status of actions. FSMs achieve this through well-defined state transitions triggered by events, including changes in sensory conditions. Hybrid systems often integrate aspects of these architectures. For instance, a robot might utilise a BT for overall mission planning and coordination, with individual leaf nodes triggering FSMs for more intricate subtasks or lower-level motor control that may have internal states.

In summary, while behaviour-based methods emphasized reactivity and distributed control, BTs and FSMs offer more structured frameworks for designing complex robotic behaviours. BTs highlight modularity, reactivity, and readability as key advantages over traditional FSMs for many robotics applications.

\subsection{Learning based Robotics Systems}
\label{sec::LiteratureReview::RobotControlLBM}

Imitation Learning (IL) enables robots to learn new skills by mimicking human behaviour \cite{xiao_robot_2024}. It is a straightforward and stable form of supervised learning where the robot learns a mapping from sensory inputs to actions based on expert demonstrations. IL can reduce the complexity of the robot strategy space and improve learning efficiency. Different forms of demonstration exist, including teleoperation, shadowing, instrumented teaching, and external observation \cite{ingrand_deliberation_2017}. Recent advancements include learning language-conditioned behaviour via imitation learning \cite{lynch_interactive_2022}. Language-Conditioned Learning involves training robots to perform tasks based on natural language instructions. This can be achieved through imitation learning, reinforcement learning, or by grounding language in robotic affordances \cite{ahn_as_2022}. Foundation models, particularly Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs), are increasingly used to enhance language understanding and planning for language-conditioned robot control. Methods like RT-1 and RT-2 utilise transformer models for large-scale, language-conditioned robotic learning \cite{brohan_rt-1_2023, brohan_rt-2_2023}. Learning from Demonstration (LfD) is also a paradigm under IL where robots learn policies from demonstrated behaviours. Approaches can learn policies, state-action dynamics, or explicit action models \cite{ingrand_deliberation_2017}. IL enables robots to learn new skills by mimicking human behaviour. It is a straightforward and stable form of supervised learning; it can be used to develop robotic systems that support human robot interaction and social awareness. However, the disadvantage is that it requires labelled behaviour data and thus needs a large amount of data to cover various human robot interactions. Even with that, the system may not be able to surpass human-level performance and may lack adaptability to new environments.

Reinforcement Learning allows robots to learn optimal strategies through trial and error by maximising a reward signal \cite{xiao_robot_2024}. Compared to IL, RL has the potential to surpass human-level performance, but it requires defining reward functions and can face challenges in exploration and convergence. Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) combines RL with deep neural networks to handle high-dimensional sensory inputs, such as images \cite{hua_learning_2021}. DRL has been applied to various robot control tasks, including manipulation and navigation. This can also be used to learn socially normative robot navigation behaviours \cite{bera_sociosense_2017}. Still, handcrafted rewards in socially aware navigation are unlikely to quantify broad social compliance effectively and can lead to reward exploitation problems \cite{wang_feedback-efficient_2022}.

Transfer Learning focuses on transferring knowledge learned in one domain (e.g., simulation) to another (e.g., the real world) to improve learning efficiency and generalisation \cite{hua_learning_2021}. Transfer learning is particularly relevant for robot manipulation, where collecting large amounts of real-world data can be challenging. Strategies for improving transfer learning include building better simulation environments, policy randomisation, and direct training of robust policies. However, it can be difficult to transfer control strategies to real robots due to issues with hardware and sensor integration.

These learning-based methods encompass a broad spectrum of techniques that enable robots to acquire skills, adapt to their environment, and execute complex tasks autonomously based on various input forms, including demonstrations, interactions, and language instructions. The integration of foundation models is a significant recent trend that leverages internet-scale data to enhance the capabilities of these learning methods.

\subsection{Robotic Systems with LLMs}
\label{sec::LiteratureReview::RobotControlLLM}

Large Language Models (LLMs) are models designed to directly output textual information in response to textual inputs, commonly referred to as prompts \cite{vaswani_attention_2023}. These models were developed for general-purpose use and can be applied to various tasks, including information retrieval and code generation. However, with additional information provided during prompting and depending on the training dataset used for training the LLM, LLMs can also be utilised for robotic control. LLMs offer numerous ways to control robots, leveraging their advanced capabilities in natural language processing for understanding, reasoning, and task planning \cite{firoozi_foundation_2023, lin_advances_2024, wang_lami_2024}.

%--- speech interface
LLMs can serve as the core of sophisticated speech interfaces for robots, which is particularly beneficial for human-robot interaction \cite{padmanabha_voicepilot_2024, Yuan_2024}. They enable users to provide high-level commands and nuanced preferences through natural language. By defining a set of robot functions (Robot API) in the prompt, LLMs can access and use these to control the robot based on voice commands. This can involve perceiving objects and grasping them, for example, in a cleaning task. LLMs can parse user intent from spoken dialogue and convert it into a sequence of high-level function calls \cite{vemprala_chatgpt_2023}. Feedback from users indicates a strong preference for the flexibility that speech interfaces powered by LLMs can offer \cite{padmanabha_voicepilot_2024}.

%--- Navigation and Control
LLMs can be used to interpret natural language instructions related to navigation and translate them into actionable plans \cite{lin_advances_2024}. Authors have shown that approaches involving verbalising the robot's task, trajectory, and visual observations can help LLMs understand navigation instructions and execute corresponding actions. Additionally, they have demonstrated that LLMs can be utilised to generate low-level control commands that directly control a robot's dynamic motion, thereby eliminating the need for task-specific fine-tuning by collecting cues from the physical environment. This enables robots to perform basic actions, such as walking, based on textual prompts \cite{lin_advances_2024, wang_prompt_2024}. Frameworks like VELMA generate the following robot action based on a contextual prompt consisting of a verbalised trajectory and visual observations \cite{kong_embodied_2024}.

%--- Path Planning
In addition to navigation and control, LLMs demonstrate potential in high-level and low-level robot path planning \cite{kong_embodied_2024, latif_3p-llm_2024}. They can solve mathematical problems relevant to path planning. Some frameworks utilise LLMs to generate waypoints for coverage path planning without extensive training data. The 3P-LLM framework demonstrates that LLMs, such as GPT-3.5-turbo, can provide real-time, adaptive, and accurate path-planning algorithms, even outperforming traditional methods like RRT and A* in processing time. However, the accuracy and path length of GPT-3.5-Turbo trail behind A* and RRT \cite{latif_3p-llm_2024}

%--- Feedback and replanning
LLMs can also incorporate environment feedback to refine their plans in a closed-loop manner \cite{huang_inner_2022, joublin_copal_2023}. The \textit{Inner Monologue} approach utilises feedback, including success detection, scene description, and human interaction, to enable LLMs to more richly process and plan execution plans, thereby significantly improving high-level instruction completion. When encountering problems, LLMs can be prompted again to resolve them. Frameworks like CoPAL (Corrective Planning of Robot Actions with Large Language Models) utilise multi-level feedback loops to enhance planning and execution, leveraging low-level feedback from the environment and task execution \cite{joublin_copal_2023}. In action failure, a robot can summarise the reason and execute replanning. Users can also provide natural language feedback to correct a robot's mistakes, allowing for online behaviour correction \cite{mahadevan_generative_2024, wang_lami_2024}.

%--- Task Plan Creation
A significant usage of LLMs for the versatile use of robots is generating task plans from natural language instructions \cite{firoozi_foundation_2023, kawaharazuka_real-world_2024, rana_sayplan_2023, lin_text2motion_2023}. This entails translating high-level user goals into sequences of executable robotic actions or skills, drawing on extensive world knowledge and reasoning capabilities of LLMs to generate effective execution plans \cite{joublin_copal_2023}. LLMs can be prompted with specifications of available actions, objects, and example programs to create sequences of actions. Methods like ProgPrompt represent robot plans as Pythonic programs, using LLMs to complete the code based on natural language instructions \cite{singh_progprompt_2022}. LLMs can also generate code for robot policy based on language commands. This code can express functions or feedback loops that process perception outputs and invoke control primitive APIs \cite{liang_code_2023}. Some approaches use LLMs to generate decision-making actions by combining information from visual foundation models and robotic skills \cite{huang_instruct2act_2023}. 

Additionally, LLMs can be used to generate expressive robot behaviours that adapt to user feedback by leveraging their understanding of social context \cite{mahadevan_generative_2024} and also models like SayCan use LLMs to score the likelihood of following action candidates from language instructions, combined with visual affordance scores to select the action to execute \cite{ahn_as_2022}.

One of the drawbacks of using LLMs is their limited direct experience with the physical world, as they are primarily trained on text data. This can lead to plans or actions that are not feasible or safe \cite{wang_prompt_2024, latif_3p-llm_2024, yu_language_2023}. Another issue is that the performance of LLMs in robotics heavily relies on well-designed prompts, including clear instructions, API definitions, and relevant examples (one-shot, few-shot capabilities) \cite{vemprala_chatgpt_2023, singh_progprompt_2022}. LLMs can sometimes generate incorrect or nonsensical outputs, which can be problematic in safety-critical robotic applications \cite{ahn_as_2022}. Directly mapping natural language to precise low-level robot actions remains a challenge due to the abstraction gap and the limited representation of low-level actions in the training data of large language models (LLMs) \cite{wang_prompt_2024, yu_language_2023}

\subsection{Robotic Systems with VLMs}
\label{sec::LiteratureReview::RobotControlVLM}

Vision-Language Models (VLMs) can be leveraged to interpret and extract relevant information from visual and textual inputs. In robotic control, this capability is particularly valuable for capturing environmental contexts often overlooked by traditional systems, such as mapping and object detection. By processing visual and linguistic data together, VLMs help bridge the gap between human language instructions and corresponding robot actions \cite{firoozi_foundation_2023}.

VLMs can enable robots to understand and execute a wide range of instructions given in natural language \cite{firoozi_foundation_2023}. This can involve generating high-level execution and even low-level motion plans responding to the user's request. For example, a robot could be instructed to "put the banana in the bowl" \cite{singh_progprompt_2022}.

VLMs can analyse visual data from robot sensors, such as cameras, to understand the environment. They can identify objects \cite{zhao_chat_2023}, understand spatial relationships \cite{huang_visual_2023}, and provide the robot with a richer understanding of the task context \cite{zhang_navid_2024}. For instance, VLMs can be used for open-vocabulary object detection.

Similar to LLMs, VLMs can also be used to generate executable robot task plans within frameworks like \textit{ProgPrompt} \cite{singh_progprompt_2022} by understanding the environment and robot capabilities described in prompts. By fusing pre-trained visual-language features with 3D reconstructions, VLMaps \cite{huang_visual_2023} can be generated, enabling natural language indexing of the environment and facilitating navigation based on complex spatial instructions.

In essence, VLMs are powerful tools that enable more natural and intelligent interaction between humans and robots by allowing robots to perceive and comprehend the world through the lenses of both vision and language. This is driving advancements in various robotic tasks, including manipulation and navigation. In the following two sections, Vision Language Models for Manipulation (VLA) and Vision Language Models for Navigation (VLN) will be discussed.

\subsection{Robotic Systems with VLAs}
\label{sec::LiteratureReview::RobotControlVLA}

Vision-Language Action (VLA) models are a class of models designed to directly output robot actions based on both visual and natural language inputs. Unlike models that only navigate (VLN) or understand vision and language (VLM), VLA models serve as end-to-end systems capable of controlling robotic manipulators and mobile bases in response to visual cues and textual commands \cite{brohan_rt-1_2023, brohan_rt-2_2023}

One of the earliest systems, RT-1 \cite{brohan_rt-1_2023}, is a transformer-based model trained on a large dataset spanning 13 robots, 130,000 episodes, and 700 tasks. It requires visual input and task descriptions to produce robot actions, including arm movements (x, y, z, roll, pitch, yaw, and gripper release) and base motions (x, y, z), along with state variables for control and termination. RT-1 demonstrates generalisation to new instructions and supports long-horizon tasks, but its limitations include restricted generalisation to unseen combinations of known concepts and limited dexterity.

Building on RT-1, RT-2 \cite{brohan_rt-2_2023} enhances the vision-language backbone by integrating web-scale data, enabling broader generalisation across objects, scenes, and instructions. It introduces capabilities such as chain-of-thought prompting and improved reasoning, although it remains constrained by the distribution of skills in its training data. The system is hosted on a multi-TPU cloud infrastructure, enabling real-time operation, but this also contributes to high computational costs, thereby limiting scalability.

Another VLA approach \cite{jiang_vima_2023} frames diverse manipulation tasks as a unified sequence modeling problem by combining multimodal prompts that incorporate visual and language cues.

Methods like SayCan, although not strictly end-to-end VLA models compared to RT-1 and RT-2, focus on grounding language instructions in a robot's affordances. They determine feasible and contextually appropriate actions based on both language understanding (LLMs) and the robot's capabilities, as learned through value functions. The selected skill is then executed. ProgPrompt also aims to generate executable plans by using LLMs with programming language features to model robot capabilities.

While not strictly end-to-end, methods like SayCan \cite{ahn_as_2022} bridge natural language understanding with robot affordances by selecting feasible actions based on value functions, which are then executed by the robot. Similarly, ProgPrompt \cite{singh_progprompt_2022} leverages LLMs with programming-like structures to generate executable plans.

Despite large datasets and robust architectures, VLA systems—such as RT-1 and RT-2—are still limited by the skills encoded in their training data. Even with increasing model size, adaptability is hampered by operational costs and execution complexity.

VLA models typically use vision-language models (VLMs) as a foundation, training them to represent actions and language as text tokens. This approach leverages large-scale pretraining on visual and linguistic data to improve performance on robotic tasks. For example, RT-2 can interpret commands like "put the strawberry into the correct bowl" by reasoning about object properties—something that goes beyond essential recognition \cite{brohan_rt-2_2023}.

However, VLA models also inherit the limitations and biases of LLMs/VLMs, including challenges with negation, ambiguity, and hallucination (incorrect outputs) \cite{ahn_as_2022, rana_sayplan_2023, kawaharazuka_real-world_2024}. Methods like SayPlan may miss opportunities for correction by excluding infeasible actions, while graph-based reasoning approaches can be restrictive in dynamic environments \cite{joublin_copal_2023}. Furthermore, effective fine-tuning still relies heavily on real-world robotic trajectory data, which is difficult and costly to collect.

In summary, VLA models mark a significant step toward integrating perception, language, and action in robotics. They enable complex manipulations and interaction with humans through end-to-end learning. Yet, they face ongoing challenges in grounding, reasoning, and scaling for real-world deployment.

The systems proposed in \cite{brohan_rt-1_2023, brohan_rt-2_2023} demonstrate that, despite using a large dataset, the skills of the resultant system are ultimately determined by the skill distribution of the training dataset. On the other hand, even as the accuracy and functionality of the robot's skills increase with model size, adaptability and usability suffer due to factors such as operational costs and execution bottlenecks.

VLA models are designed to map robot observations (primarily visual) and natural language instructions directly to executable robot actions \cite{brohan_rt-1_2023, brohan_rt-2_2023}. This is achieved by training models to represent actions as text tokens and incorporating them into the training set alongside natural language tokens and visual information, such as images or videos. To achieve this, a VLM model pre-trained on language and vision-language data is typically used as a foundation and then improved or fine-tuned, leveraging robot observations to inform actions and benefit from the pre-training on language and vision-language data.

Since most VLA models are based on LLMs or VLMs, they also inherit the limitations and biases of the underlying models, including potential inaccuracies and difficulties with tasks like negation and ambiguous references \cite{ahn_as_2022, rana_sayplan_2023}. SayPlan, for instance, is constrained by the graph-based reasoning limitations of LLMs. Additionally, while web pretraining helps, effective fine-tuning of VLA models still requires robotic trajectory data, which can be difficult to collect due to robot limitations \cite{brohan_rt-2_2023}. Grounding language in the physical world, especially in environments with combinatorial action spaces, remains a challenge. Even methods like SayCan can be limited by the range and robustness of the underlying skills. Some approaches might exclude non-feasible skills from a plan, potentially missing opportunities for corrective actions \cite{joublin_copal_2023}. Language models can sometimes produce incorrect outputs, known as hallucinations, which can impact the reliability of VLA model actions \cite{kawaharazuka_real-world_2024}

In essence, VLA models represent a promising direction in robotics by directly integrating visual perception, language understanding, and action execution through large-scale pretraining and end-to-end learning. However, they still face challenges related to the limitations of their underlying models, the complexities of real-world environments, and the need for robust grounding and reasoning capabilities.

\subsection{Robotics Systems with VLNs}
\label{sec::LiteratureReview::RobotControlVLN}

Vision-Language Navigation (VLN) is a research area that focuses on enabling mobile agents to navigate to a goal location through diverse, often unseen environments by following free-form, natural language instructions and visual information captured through devices \cite{zhang_navid_2024, majumdar_improving_2020}, thereby mimicking human navigation. To achieve this functionality, the agent must be able to comprehend complex visual observations and interpret fine-grained instructions. VLN can be formulated as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) where the agent, at each time step, receives a visual observation and must plan a low-level action based on a natural language instruction.

Research in VLN has explored both simplified, discrete environments (using connectivity graphs) and more complex continuous environments. In discrete settings, navigation is often cast as teleportation between waypoints. Continuous environments involve planning low-level actions based on visual input \cite{zhang_navid_2024}. Key challenges in VLN include generalisation to unseen environments and bridging the gap between simulation and real-world applications. Effectively understanding the visual-textual correspondence for decision-making and maintaining a sense of navigation progress over potentially long instructions are also critical challenges \cite{hong_recurrent_2021}.

Due to VLN's complexity, recent research leverages large vision-language models (VLMs) and large language models (LLMs) to enhance navigation capabilities. These models can provide firm visual grounding and common-sense reasoning for better instruction, following, and planning. Some works focus on building spatial representations, such as Visual Language Maps (VLMaps), to ground language in the environment.

One benefit of Video-based VLMs is that they can effectively encode past visual observations as spatio-temporal contexts, which can be used for future decision-making and instruction following \cite{zhang_navid_2024}. VLN models, particularly those that leverage VLMs, demonstrate potential for superior cross-dataset and Sim2Real transfer by utilising large-scale pre-training on web data and navigation samples. \cite{zhang_navid_2024}, for instance, has been shown state-of-the-art performance in both simulation and real-world environments without relying on maps or depth sensors. Integrating large foundation models, such as VLMs and LLMs, brings strong prior knowledge and generalisation capabilities to VLN tasks, enabling zero-shot or few-shot learning for navigation. These models can aid in understanding complex instructions, reasoning about the environment, and planning appropriate actions.

\cite{chen_open-vocabulary_2022, huang_visual_2023, lin_advances_2024, kawaharazuka_real-world_2024} focuses on creating queryable scene representations or semantic maps that can be indexed with natural language, allowing for more complex spatial reasoning and goal localisation.

Ultimately, VLN remains a challenging task due to the need to comprehend both complex visual inputs and nuanced natural language instructions, which requires sophisticated cross-modal understanding and reasoning. This complexity increases when working in real-world environments, which are continuous action spaces, and in cluttered spaces due to the requirement of grounding textual information with visual elements \cite{zhang_navid_2024}. Additionally, since VLNs are primarily based on VLMs, issues related to VLMs can also impact VLNs. This may include limitations in basic spatial reasoning, directional understanding, and accurate localisation, hindering their direct applicability in all navigation scenarios.

As inherent with all foundational models, adequate training of VLN agents still requires substantial embodied path-instruction data, which can be costly and time-consuming to collect \cite{majumdar_improving_2020, lin_adapt_2022}. The diversity of objects and situations in existing datasets may also be insufficient to meet all real-world needs \cite{lin_advances_2024}.
