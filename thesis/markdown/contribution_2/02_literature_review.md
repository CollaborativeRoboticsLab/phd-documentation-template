# Literature Review

Pre-Execution Verification of Adaptive Robotic Task and Behavior Plans: A Comparative Analysis of Formal, Stochastic, and Generative FrameworksTheoretical Foundations and the Evolutionary Shift in Robotic PlanningRobotic task and behavior planning has undergone a fundamental transition from rigid, deterministic models to highly adaptive, context-aware frameworks. Classical planning systems typically represent the physical world through symbolic descriptions, most notably utilizing the Planning Domain Definition Language (PDDL). In these traditional systems, an engineer must exhaustively define the domain, including all environmental states, action operators, preconditions, and post-action effects. While these non-generative, symbolic methods provide strong logical guarantees, they are inherently limited by their inability to scale to open-world environments, adapt to dynamic real-world changes, or interpret ambiguous natural language commands without intensive human intervention.The introduction of Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs) has provided a mechanism to bypass these modeling constraints. By leveraging the extensive commonsense reasoning and semantic knowledge embedded within foundation models, modern robotic architectures can translate high-level natural language instructions directly into executable action sequences. This capability allows robots to dynamically adjust their behavior plans based on situational context, environment modifications, or user feedback.Despite this flexibility, generative task planners introduce significant reliability risks. LLMs lack inherent spatial-temporal reasoning and physical grounding, frequently generating plan sequences that appear plausible but contain critical flaws. These anomalies typically manifest as position errors, missing physical prerequisites, or redundant and physically impossible actions, such as instructing a manipulator to pour water into a closed container or step into a room before opening the corresponding door.Consequently, pre-execution verification has emerged as a crucial layer in modern robotics, serving to mathematically or logically validate generated behaviors before they are deployed on physical hardware. This verification boundary bridges the gap between high-level generative flexibility and low-level physical safety.Formal Verification Methodologies Without Large Language ModelsPrior to the integration of generative artificial intelligence, robotic verification relied on formal mathematical models. These classical techniques utilize model checking and theorem proving to exhaustively evaluate a system's state space against mathematically defined specifications, ensuring absolute correctness under the modeled assumptions.Real-Time Schedulability and Constraint Verification via UPPAALThe UPPAAL tool suite is a leading framework for modeling, validating, and verifying real-time systems represented as networks of timed automata. Timed automata extend traditional finite-state machines by incorporating dense-time clock variables that progress synchronously, allowing the system to capture temporal constraints and schedulability criteria. Within UPPAAL, a timed automaton is formally defined as a tuple:$$(L, l_0, C, A, E, I)$$where $L$ represents a finite set of locations, $l_0 \in L$ is the initial location, $C$ is the set of real-valued clocks, $A$ is the set of actions, $E \subseteq L \times B(C) \times A \times 2^C \times L$ is the set of edges defining transitions, and $I: L \rightarrow B(C)$ maps each location to a clock invariant that limits the time a system can remain in that location.The model checker evaluates these timed automata networks by converting verification queries into symbolic state space representation constraints, checking properties such as reachability, safety invariants, liveness, and the complete absence of deadlocks. To demonstrate the academic and industrial breadth of this framework, a systematic PRISMA literature review of UPPAAL applications spanning the years 2022 and 2023 examined 188 distinct studies, highlighting its deployment across diverse domains, including autonomous transport, communication protocols, and real-time robotic intersection control.Over its development, the UPPAAL family has blossomed into specialized, domain-specific engines. UPPAAL CORA addresses cost-optimal reachability analysis, enabling planners to find paths that minimize execution cost or time. UPPAAL TIGA acts as a timed game solver, synthesizing control strategies in the presence of uncontrollable environmental adversaries. UPPAAL SMC (Statistical Model Checking) provides an alternative to exhaustive state-space exploration. SMC monitors simulated execution runs of stochastic networks of timed automata, applying statistical metrics such as sequential hypothesis testing and Monte Carlo simulations to determine whether a system satisfies properties with a specified degree of confidence. UPPAAL Stratego builds upon these capabilities by facilitating the generation, optimization, and comparison of control strategies for stochastic priced timed games, maintaining these synthesized strategies as first-class objects within the verification query language.Probabilistic Modeling and Verification via PRISMIn highly dynamic or unpredictable environments where actions have stochastic outcomes, the PRISM probabilistic model checker is widely used. PRISM models autonomous missions using formalisms such as discrete-time Markov chains (DTMC), Markov decision processes (MDP), continuous-time Markov chains (CTMC), or turn-based stochastic games (SMG).The system's behavior is verified against properties specified in Probabilistic Computation Tree Logic (PCTL) or Probabilistic Linear Temporal Logic (PLTL). A standard PCTL formulation evaluates properties using the following syntax:$$\Phi ::= \text{true} \mid ap \mid \Phi \wedge \Phi \mid \neg \Phi \mid P_{\sim p}(\Psi)$$$$\Psi ::= X \Phi \mid \Phi U^{\le t} \Phi$$where $ap$ represents atomic environmental propositions, $\sim \in \{<, \le, >, \ge\}$, $p \in [0,1]$ is a probability threshold, $t \in \mathbb{N}$ is a discrete time bound, $X$ is the temporal "Next" operator, and $U$ is the "Until" operator.This quantitative modeling allows researchers to compute the minimum or maximum probability of mission success, estimate expected wait times, or calculate the probability of catastrophic failures. For instance, a foraging robot swarm can be modeled in PRISM by defining states representing resting, searching, and homing, and then verifying the probability of finding food given actuator failure rates and searching energy consumption rates.To mitigate the state-space explosion that occurs when modeling multi-robot teams, compositional verification frameworks have been developed. By representing cooperative robot teams as parallel probabilistic automata ($\alpha\text{PAs}$), researchers have proved that the probability of satisfying LTL specifications accumulates multiplicatively across independent teams, while expected rewards, such as area coverage, accumulate additively. This compositional approach allows verification engines to evaluate individual components in isolation, maintaining tractability as the robot count increases.Foundation Model-Driven and Neuro-Symbolic Verification FrameworksWith the introduction of LLMs and VLMs into robotic task planning, researchers have developed neuro-symbolic verification methodologies. These approaches combine the commonsense reasoning of foundation models with the mathematical structure of formal verification to validate and adapt robot behavior plans before execution.The VerifyLLM ArchitectureThe VerifyLLM framework combines LLMs with Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) to systematically detect and correct inconsistencies in generated action sequences. In environments with complex physical constraints, high-level task planning and low-level motion planning must be solved jointly, making isolated sub-goal decomposition ineffective.Rather than using an LLM to plan trajectories directly, AutoTAMP uses a few-shot LLM translator to map natural language task descriptions into an intermediate task representation. This intermediate representation is then verified and processed by a traditional TAMP solver to compute symbolic actions and collision-free joint trajectories.To ensure the validity of the translated specifications, AutoTAMP employs automated syntactic and semantic checkers. If an error is detected, the framework initiates an autoregressive re-prompting loop, feeding the error back to the LLM for self-correction.Evaluating these hybrid models is computationally intensive; systematic testing across environments such as Chip's Challenge, HouseWorld, Overcooked, Rover, and Wall required two weeks of continuous execution on four 16-core CPUs, with API costs totaling approximately 1500 USD.In parallel, the CaStL (Constraints as Specifications through LLMs) framework uses LLMs to translate natural language instructions into four distinct constraint categories: attribute, eventual, implication, and global constraints. These translated constraints are integrated into a standard TAMP solver, which restricts the search space to trajectories that are kinematically feasible and physically safe, reducing execution failures.Other hybrid models include LLM-Flax, which generates PDDL relaxation rules and complementary constraints via structured prompting, incorporating LLM-guided failure recovery to eliminate manual engineering. Onto-LLM-TAMP incorporates ontological reasoning, querying domain knowledge bases to automatically append semantic constraints to LLM prompts (e.g., prioritizing placing crockery before food). Similarly, VeriGraph integrates Vision-Language Models with 3D spatial scene graphs (3DSGs) to construct execution-verifiable plans, grounding verification in semantic and topological environmental contexts.Resource-Based Verification: Time, Energy, Known Failures, and Success RatesTo ensure real-world viability, pre-execution verification must evaluate physical resource constraints, specifically temporal limits, electrical energy consumption, potential failure modes, and expected success rates.Quantitative Temporal ModelingIn temporal planning, time serves as a critical constraint and optimization metric. UPPAAL models this using dense-time clocks to verify real-time deadlines and schedulability properties.For robots navigating dynamic environments, spatio-temporal models can estimate both the navigation success probability and the expected transition time between topological map nodes based on the time of day. These predictions are incorporated into a Markov Decision Process (MDP) to generate time-optimal routing policies.Furthermore, feature importance analyses of machine learning models trained to predict robotic resources indicate that processing time has a high variance reduction score (approximately 0.284), making it a key feature in estimating overall operational duration.Actuator Energy Consumption and Efficiency ProfilesRobotic energy consumption is a critical optimization target, particularly for battery-powered mobile platforms and industrial manipulators. Historically, multi-robot task allocation (MRTA) has optimized task sequences using genetic algorithms (GAs) and A* search, minimizing a combined cost function:$$\text{SumCost} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} C_i$$where $C_i$ represents the operational cost of robot $i$.To avoid individual battery depletion and the "weakest link" effect in robot swarms, researchers implement energy penalty strategies. These models calculate the average energy consumption of the swarm:$$\bar{E}_X = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} E(R_i)$$where $E(R_i)$ is the energy consumed by robot $i$. The penalty term, based on the deviation of a robot's consumption from this average, is integrated into the genetic algorithm's fitness function to distribute workloads and balance the swarm's state of charge (SoC).Predicting and verifying these energy metrics from high-level operational plans is challenging. Standard machine learning models (such as Random Forest or Linear Regression) trained on high-level operational descriptors (e.g., total processing time, task category) show minimal predictive improvements over basic mean-based baselines. This is because high-level plan steps fail to capture the low-level physical and joint-level dynamics that dictate actuator energy draw.To address this, researchers propose simplified differentiative robot energy models using inverse inertial dynamics and single electrical parameters to optimize trajectories before deployment.For collaborative robots (cobots), physical profiling reveals distinct energy behaviors compared to traditional industrial robots:Traditional industrial robots show a parabolic energy-to-time curve where very slow or very fast movements increase energy consumption, making intermediate speeds ($T_{opt}$) optimal.Collaborative robots operate in a slow-motion, gravity-compensated zone where the power needed to compensate for gravity dominates over the power needed for acceleration.Consequently, maximizing both speed and acceleration limits within safe ranges reduces the trajectory time and minimizes total energy consumption, though it results in higher peak power draws.These profiles allow Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) and Integrated Genetic Algorithms (IGA) to optimize task sequencing and trajectory execution. Spatio-temporal models are used to estimate transition probabilities and navigation durations in topological maps based on the time of day. These probabilities populate a Markov Decision Process (MDP) to generate optimal plans.If a plan cannot be achieved with absolute certainty, standard value iteration algorithms may fail to converge. To resolve this, researchers implement partial satisfiability frameworks. By defining a task progression function over co-safe LTL formulas, the verification engine constructs a lexicographic reward structure that prioritizes system actions in a hierarchical order:Maximize the probability of fully satisfying the co-safe LTL task specification.Maximize the task progression reward (accomplishing as much of the task as possible) if full satisfaction is unreachable.Minimize the expected execution time or operational cost to achieve the preceding objectives.To handle the parametric uncertainty of extreme environments, runtime verification frameworks use Bayesian estimators to learn task completion and failure rates. Bayesian Inference using Partial Priors (BIPP) estimates bounded intervals for the occurrence of singular events (e.g., catastrophic physical failures). Simultaneously, Bayesian Inference using Imprecise Probability with Sets of Priors (IPSP) estimates the rates of regularly encountered operational transitions.These learned parameters define an interval continuous-time Markov model. Robust probabilistic model checkers, such as PRISM-PSY, verify this model to compute bounded intervals for success probability and expected energy usage, capturing the epistemic uncertainty of the mission.For learned multi-agent coordination policies, direct verification of neural network execution is mathematically intractable. To verify these policies, researchers extract features from agent observations and distill high-dimensional neural policies into decision trees using algorithms like CART, achieving high fidelity (e.g., $97.9\% \pm 1.2\%$).The synthesized decision tree is compiled into a DTMC specification for compositional PCTL verification via the PRISM model checker. In multi-drone coordination tests, this pipeline verified safety, liveness, and cooperation properties, maintaining a collision rate of 0.3% (below the 1.0% safety threshold). This discrete policy distillation approach also enabled a 3x to 4x speedup in verification times compared to continuous communication methods.Verification-Driven Replanning, Error Correction, and ReconfigurationPre-execution verification acts as an active feedback loop. When a planning failure, resource violation, or logical inconsistency is detected, the verification engine triggers automated corrections or replanning.LLM-Guided and Autoregressive CorrectionsIn hybrid generative frameworks, correction loops operate by dynamically re-prompting the LLM.In the AutoTAMP pipeline, if the syntactic or semantic checkers identify errors in the translated intermediate representations, the system automatically appends the error trace to the context window. The LLM performs autoregressive self-correction, re-generating the intermediate task representation until it passes all checks.LLM3 incorporates continuous motion planning feedback directly into the planning loop. If the motion planner fails to generate a collision-free path or find valid joint parameters, the motion planner sends a natural language error log back to the LLM. The LLM evaluates the motion planning failure and refines the symbolic action sequence or parameters (e.g., choosing a different grasp pose).VerifyLLM identifies LTL violations within its sliding window and automatically adjusts the plan. If it flags a missing prerequisite, it inserts the required action; if it identifies redundant actions, it prunes them. This automatic refinement ensures the final action sequence matches the logical structure of the task.Stochastic and Schedulability ReschedulingIn non-generative systems, planning corrections rely on control strategy synthesis and dynamic rescheduling.UPPAAL Stratego synthesizes optimal control policies for stochastic priced timed games. When environmental disruptions occur, Stratego-derived controllers adapt the robot's actions, maintaining real-time safety and resource constraints.For multi-robot swarms, Integrated Genetic Algorithms use event-triggered dynamic rescheduling. If a robot experiences rapid battery depletion, the system triggers dynamic rescheduling to reallocate remaining tasks, balancing the swarm's state of charge.In single-agent topological navigation, if path navigation fails (e.g., an obstacle blocks a hallway), lexicographic MDP models synthesize fallback paths to maximize remaining task progress.For safety-critical missions, self-certifying systems continuously monitor runtime execution. If the verified intervals for success probability fall below a safety threshold, the system triggers dynamic assurance reconfigurations to adapt the plan.Comparative Assessment of Task Planning and space before physical executionConclusions and Strategic RecommendationsThe transition from rigid, manually compiled symbolic models to adaptive, generative task planning represents a major step forward for autonomous robotics. However, this flexibility introduces safety challenges, making pre-execution verification a necessity rather than an optional safeguard.A review of traditional formal methods and generative neuro-symbolic verification frameworks suggests several key trends:The Necessity of Hybrid Neuro-Symbolic Architectures: Relying solely on generative models for task planning is insufficient for safety-critical missions. Coupling LLM-based planning with formal logic constraints (such as LTL translation in VerifyLLM or intermediate representations in AutoTAMP) provides a mathematically sound sandbox that preserves generative flexibility while guaranteeing safety.The Importance of Kinematic and Resource Co-Design: Verification cannot treat high-level symbolic plans and low-level physical dynamics as separate problems. High-level plans must be evaluated against physical limitations, including joint limits, load capacity, and battery-discharge profiles. For collaborative platforms, maximizing speed and acceleration within safe operating limits often minimizes total energy consumption, which must be factored into task planning.The Transition to Continuous Runtime Verification: Pre-execution verification is a necessary first step but must be paired with self-certifying runtime verification. Combining Bayesian learning models with interval continuous-time Markov models allows robots to dynamically verify and update mission success probabilities and energy usage in response to real-world deviations.For researchers and developers designing autonomous robotic systems, the following recommendations are proposed:Implement a hybrid architecture where natural language is translated into intermediate formal representations (such as LTL or PDDL) rather than direct robot instructions, allowing verification before execution.Incorporate low-level geometric and kinematic constraints directly into symbolic task planning using constraint-based TAMP frameworks to prevent execution-level failures.Integrate probabilistic model checking with online Bayesian estimators to enable real-time risk assessment, allowing autonomous platforms to safely adapt to changing environments.
