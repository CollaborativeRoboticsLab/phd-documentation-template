# Fault Tolerance at the Cognitive Level

The integration of foundation models into robotics changes both the scope and the meaning of fault tolerance. Classical robotic reliability was built around explicitly modelled worlds, bounded disturbances, and well-defined operational envelopes. In that setting, failures could often be attributed to sensor noise, state-estimation drift, controller instability, or hardware degradation. Once large language models, vision-language models, and vision-language-action systems are introduced, robotic failure acquires a cognitive dimension. A system may now fail because it misinterprets intent, hallucinates an object or affordance, loses track of state across a long reasoning chain, or produces an action sequence that is linguistically plausible but physically incoherent. Reliability in such systems therefore depends not only on physical robustness but also on runtime cognitive vigilance. [A Survey of Robot Intelligence with Large Language Models]

This shift is easiest to see by distinguishing different ways foundation models are embedded into robotic pipelines. When a language model acts as a high-level planner, it decomposes natural-language goals into symbolic actions or code-like plans, and failure tends to appear as logical inconsistency, syntax error, or infeasibility with respect to the robot's embodiment. When a vision-language model is used primarily for perception, failure more often takes the form of hallucinated objects, attribute confusion, or incorrect affordance interpretation. End-to-end vision-language-action models create a different challenge again: they offer strong generalization but obscure where failure originates, making it difficult to tell whether the problem is perceptual, semantic, geometric, or motoric without inspecting internal representations.

Cognitive fault tolerance begins with a taxonomy of these new failure modes. Hallucination is one core category, but in robotics it is more consequential than in pure text systems because it can directly trigger unsafe or impossible actions. A model may contradict available sensory evidence, infer nonexistent environmental facts, or correctly identify an object semantically while misunderstanding its physical accessibility or grasp affordance. Another category is reasoning drift, where an extended chain of planning gradually loses logical consistency or forgets the consequences of earlier actions. Semantic misalignment forms a related class in which the robot performs the commanded motion correctly yet violates the user's actual intent, for example by optimizing a literal but inappropriate interpretation of a vague instruction. Cognitive systems also introduce adversarial vulnerabilities such as prompt injection, in which a malicious or poorly constrained input manipulates the planner into bypassing safety rules.

Because these failures are probabilistic and often latent, uncertainty quantification becomes a central requirement. Recent work argues that a single confidence score is not enough. Frameworks such as CURE instead decompose uncertainty into interpretable components, distinguishing uncertainty caused by ambiguous instructions from uncertainty caused by unfamiliar environments or from the irreducible variability of physical execution. This decomposition matters because different kinds of uncertainty demand different responses: ambiguous goals may require clarification dialogue, unfamiliar scenes may require cautious behaviour or human oversight, and high physical uncertainty may require a conservative execution policy. The broader trend in the literature is toward calibrated uncertainty estimates that can trigger intervention rather than merely describing model confidence after the fact. [Towards Reliable LLM-based Robot Planning via Combined Uncertainty Estimation]

Conformal prediction has become particularly important in this context because it provides statistical guarantees around failure detection. Methods such as SAFE and FAIL-Detect apply conformal ideas to internal policy features or execution signals, constructing thresholds that distinguish familiar successful trajectories from emerging failures with controlled error rates. This makes the monitoring problem more tractable for large learned policies, especially when explicit symbolic models are unavailable. A related line of work explores more interpretable latent structures, for example through Bayesian or sparsity-constrained decision layers, in order to improve both uncertainty calibration and human understanding of why the system appears uncertain. [SAFE paper] [FAIL-Detect paper]

Once uncertainty is quantified, the next challenge is diagnosis. The literature reveals two broad strategies. One uses external multimodal observers, often vision-language models, to examine what the robot appears to be doing and judge whether execution has gone wrong. Frameworks such as Guardian and I-FailSense exemplify this approach by analysing multi-view imagery and comparing the observed final state against the intended task. These systems are valuable because they can detect semantic errors that traditional success metrics would miss, such as placing the wrong object in the right place or achieving a stable but semantically inverted spatial relation. The second strategy monitors internal policy features directly, using lightweight regressors on frozen representations to detect the onset of failure before it becomes visually obvious. This often sacrifices semantic richness for speed, but it is better aligned with real-time robotic requirements. [Guardian paper] [I-FailSense paper]

The scarcity of failure data is a major obstacle across both approaches. Many robotic learning pipelines are built from expert demonstrations or successful rollouts, leaving failure underrepresented despite its importance for safety. Synthetic failure-generation frameworks therefore play a growing role by perturbing trajectories in simulation, labelling failure modes, and sometimes even generating candidate recovery actions. This does not solve the sim-to-real gap, but it provides the structured negative examples needed to train monitors and recovery policies. The key limitation is that simulated failures rarely capture the full multimodal messiness of real deployments, where lighting changes, contact uncertainties, network delays, and human interaction all combine. [FailSafe paper]

Recovery at the cognitive level is increasingly treated as a hierarchy spanning reflexive interruption and reflective reasoning. Some systems rely on verbal self-reflection, where a model analyses the cause of failure, stores that explanation in memory, and conditions the next attempt on the retrieved lesson. Others use generative imagination or world models to propose an alternative path back to a successful trajectory. Behaviour-tree-based supervisory architectures provide a more structured integration point, allowing learned or language-based modules to insert or choose recovery subtrees while remaining inside an interpretable execution structure. Knowledge-graph-based methods add persistence by retaining explicit records of earlier failures, object properties, and environmental constraints that can shape later recovery decisions. [RoboReflect paper] [VLA-in-the-Loop paper] [STAR paper]

Despite this diversity of recovery strategies, safety still depends on neuro-symbolic guardrails. Translation-based verification methods convert natural-language tasks or model-generated plans into formal specifications such as linear temporal logic or signal temporal logic and then check whether candidate behaviours satisfy those constraints before execution. Runtime safety layers such as control barrier functions provide a complementary mechanism by enforcing safe state boundaries even when the cognitive planner proposes unsafe motions. Other approaches attempt to learn latent verification spaces in which safe and unsafe plans become rapidly separable, trading some formal rigor for faster deployment. At the broadest level, constitutional or policy-level prompt constraints act as heuristic filters that exclude ethically or physically unacceptable tasks before lower-level planning begins. [VerifyLLM paper] [RoboGuard paper] [RepV paper] [AutoRT paper]

Two practical constraints dominate this literature. The first is the sim-to-real gap. Failure detectors and recovery policies trained in simulation often assume cleaner dynamics and more structured visual conditions than those encountered on physical robots. As a result, models that appear robust in benchmark environments can degrade sharply when transferred to real systems. The second is latency. Cognitive reasoning can take hundreds of milliseconds or seconds, while many robotic safety interventions must happen in milliseconds. This forces architectures toward layered designs in which fast statistical or feature-based monitors interrupt unsafe execution while slower vision-language or language-only modules perform deeper diagnosis and replanning asynchronously. [LAMARS paper]

Overall, the literature points toward cognitive fault tolerance as a hybrid neuro-symbolic hierarchy. Fast monitors provide early anomaly detection, formal or semi-formal safety layers constrain the space of admissible actions, and slower reasoning systems diagnose failures and generate semantically informed recovery plans. The most significant open problems remain the lack of standardized cognitive reliability benchmarks, the difficulty of calibrating uncertainty across diverse embodiments and tasks, and the challenge of integrating multimodal reflection with real-time safety. For robots that rely increasingly on generative models, dependable autonomy will depend on making these cognitive safeguards as systematic as the physical fault-tolerance mechanisms that preceded them.
