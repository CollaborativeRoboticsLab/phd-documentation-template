# Fault Tolerance at the High Level

High-level fault tolerance concerns mission-level resilience: the ability of a robotic system to revise task structure, switch strategies, and reason about failure conditions that cannot be solved by local control or simple component restarts. At this level, the question is no longer whether a signal is noisy or whether a driver has crashed, but whether the robot can still accomplish its task safely when plans fail, assumptions change, or unexpected events invalidate its current strategy. The literature consistently shows that architectural choice strongly shapes this capability.

One of the most important shifts in high-level control has been the move from finite state machines to behaviour trees. Finite state machines remain useful for tightly scoped logic, but they scale poorly as failure handling becomes richer. Every new recovery condition tends to require additional transitions across many existing states, creating a dense and fragile control graph. This transition explosion makes complex failure handling hard to maintain and hard to verify, especially when state labels begin to encode both task progress and recovery history. Behaviour trees address the same problem through modular control flow. Recovery is expressed locally through selectors, fallbacks, and reactive sequences, allowing a failed behaviour to be paired with a targeted alternative without restructuring the entire decision graph. Because conditions are re-evaluated continuously, behaviour trees also offer a more reactive response to changes in battery state, path feasibility, or precondition validity during execution. \cite{ghzouli_behavior_2023,iovino_programming_2022}

The same concern for structured recovery appears in automated planning. Symbolic planners based on PDDL remain attractive for long-horizon tasks because they generate explicit action sequences toward goal states, but fault tolerance depends on how these plans are repaired when execution diverges from expectation. Replanning from scratch is often simple to implement, yet it can be computationally expensive and can destabilize behaviour by replacing an otherwise acceptable plan with a substantially different one after a small disturbance. Plan-repair methods instead try to preserve commitment to the original strategy by modifying only the affected segment. Probabilistic planning extends this further by representing action outcomes stochastically and generating contingency-aware policies rather than single linear sequences, making failure handling part of the plan representation itself.

Hierarchical task networks offer a related but distinct route to resilience. By decomposing tasks into methods and subtasks, HTN planners can recover from failure by selecting alternative decompositions rather than reconstructing the entire task from scratch. If one method becomes infeasible because of weather, resource loss, or environmental change, a different method can be substituted while preserving the larger task intent. This makes HTN-based recovery particularly useful when the robot must preserve semantic commitment to a mission even though the operational details must change.

Recent work extends these symbolic approaches through neuro-symbolic architectures that place large language models into the high-level recovery loop. In such systems, language models may act as semantic monitors, exception handlers, or replanning agents when failures occur that were not fully modelled in the symbolic domain. This is attractive because language models can interpret logs, human feedback, and contextual descriptions that are difficult to encode manually. However, the same flexibility introduces a new verification problem: the model that proposes recovery may hallucinate actions, violate resource constraints, or produce plans that are syntactically valid but operationally unsafe. Many systems therefore embed the language model inside an exceptional-handling loop that filters outputs against admissible action sets or re-prompts the model using structured error feedback. [LLM-based Robot Task Planning with Exceptional Handling for General Purpose Service Robots]

Closed-loop reflection frameworks push this further by treating failure as an iterative reasoning problem. After a failed action, the model receives feedback about what went wrong and proposes a revised strategy, such as changing grasp force, selecting a different object approach, or using an alternative recovery skill. This turns high-level fault tolerance into a cycle of plan generation, execution, reflection, and correction. While such approaches are promising for open-ended tasks, they remain limited by latency, model inconsistency, and the lack of guarantees that the revised plan will satisfy formal safety constraints.

This is why formal methods remain central even in learning-oriented high-level architectures. Linear temporal logic and related specification languages allow planners or supervisors to express invariants, ordering constraints, and liveness conditions that recovery plans must obey. Reactive synthesis can, in principle, construct controllers that are correct by construction under stated assumptions, while counter-strategy analysis can expose why a specification is unrealizable and what assumptions would need to be refined. In practical robotic systems, these techniques increasingly serve as guardrails around adaptive planning rather than as complete replacements for it.

The high-level literature therefore suggests a convergence rather than a replacement story. Behaviour trees provide modular execution and local recovery; symbolic planners and HTN methods provide structured task repair; language models provide flexibility in the face of unmodelled situations; and formal specifications provide the safety envelope within which adaptation is permitted. The main unresolved challenges concern trustworthiness, latency, and integration. Language-model-based recovery remains difficult to verify, high-latency inference is still misaligned with fast failure response, and there is no standard recovery interface that spans navigation, manipulation, perception, and semantic reasoning. These limitations make unified high-level health management a significant open problem for dependable robotic autonomy.
