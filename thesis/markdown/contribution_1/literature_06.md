## Behaviour Representations in Robotics

### Skill and capability representations in robotics

Representing robot skills, tasks, behaviours, and capabilities has been a recurring problem in robotics, especially when the goal is to build systems that can be reused, inspected, and composed across different applications. Prior work spans rigid instructive systems, which are often easy to reason about but hard to adapt, and more flexible representations that better accommodate real-world variation but can be difficult to ground symbolically \cite{chen2024language, olivares2019review}. For HRI systems, this representation problem matters directly because interaction quality depends on the robot exposing understandable behaviour interfaces while remaining responsive to changing contexts.

Many contemporary robot control systems organise behaviour hierarchically, with tasks decomposed into skills and lower-level actions \cite{mayr2023skiros2}. Behaviour Trees (BTs) and Hierarchical Finite State Machines (HFSMs) are two widely used patterns for orchestrating such behaviour \cite{colledanchise2021implementation, ghzouli2023behavior}. Frameworks such as FlexBe, SkiROS2, and KnowRob combine these execution structures with explicit knowledge representations, often using RDF- or OWL-style ontologies to relate skills, tasks, and world state \cite{schillinger2016human, mayr2023skiros2, tenorth2009knowrob, van2004owl, pantano2022capability}. These systems demonstrate the value of formal skill representations, but they also reveal how fragmented the space remains.

### Research gap between symbolic and adaptive systems

A recent review of capability-based robot frameworks highlighted an ongoing divide between industrial capability engineering approaches and academic robotics approaches \cite{pantano2022capability}. Differences in complexity, parameterisation, and standardisation make it difficult to transfer methods across platforms and domains. This becomes a practical obstacle in HRI, where research groups often need reusable yet adaptable robot interfaces without requiring deep re-engineering of the underlying subsystems.

At the same time, machine learning and generative AI have expanded the range of possible high-level robot control strategies. Hybrid approaches increasingly combine symbolic interfaces with subsymbolic reasoning methods such as deep learning or language-based planning \cite{ahmad2023learning, chen2024language, mayr2022skill}. These methods offer greater flexibility, but they also raise concerns about determinism, safety, and explainability, particularly in human-shared environments \cite{lecun2015deep, yanco2004classifying}. The literature therefore points toward a need for an intermediate representation layer that remains explicit enough for dependable control while being flexible enough to support runtime adaptation and AI-assisted planning.

### The original Capabilities package as prior art

The ROS(1) Capabilities package is an important precursor because it offered a lightweight mechanism for representing what a robot can do and how those functions can be invoked \cite{woodall2014ros}. It described capabilities through interfaces, providers, semantic interfaces, and remappings, while a capabilities server managed launch-time execution. That design made it possible to expose robot functions in a user-friendly way and reduced startup overhead by launching providers on demand.

However, the original package reflected the assumptions of the ROS(1) ecosystem and did not directly address newer needs such as composable ROS2 systems, runtime registration of capabilities, persistent capability state, or plugin-based execution strategies. Contribution 1 responds to this gap by preserving the useful abstraction of the original Capabilities package while redesigning it for ROS2, extensibility, and contemporary HRI requirements.

### Behaviour Trees and LLM-assisted planning

The earlier thesis draft also framed this contribution against a more specific planning problem: how to connect behaviour-based robot control architectures with large language models in a way that preserves grounding and execution compatibility. Behaviour Trees are a particularly relevant reference point because they are widely used to structure reusable robot behaviours and increasingly appear in systems that support automatic task generation \cite{cao_robot_nodate, colledanchise2021implementation, biggar_principled_2021}.

BTs are attractive because they support modular composition, reuse of subtrees, and reactive execution. Recent work has explored generating BTs with genetic programming, learning-from-demonstration pipelines, autonomous planners, and LLMs \cite{styrud_automatic_2024, izzo_btgenbot_2024, cao_robot_nodate}. Frameworks such as ReBeT and PlanSys2 further show how behaviour-structured execution can be integrated into larger robotic software systems \cite{alberts_rebet_2024, martin_plansys2_2021}. These developments make behaviour-based control a natural partner for capability-oriented interfaces.

At the same time, the literature identifies several failure modes when LLMs are used to generate behaviour structures directly. Generated trees can become monolithic and difficult to maintain, plans can reference behaviours that do not exist on the target robot, and hallucinated steps can undermine safety or usability \cite{anguelov_behavior_nodate, ahn_as_2022, kawaharazuka_real-world_2024}. Even when the generated plan is structurally plausible, translating it into grounded robot actions still depends on a well-defined library of skills whose parameters, constraints, and semantics are explicit \cite{vemprala_chatgpt_2023, lin_text2motion_2023}.

This is where the thesis-specific framing of Contribution 1 becomes important. Capabilities2 does not itself solve automatic planning, but it provides the explicit skill layer that makes planning systems easier to ground. The additional Prompt Tools and Capabilities2 Fabric material retained from the LaTeX chapter shows one proposed path for using that capability layer inside a larger LLM-assisted behaviour-generation stack. In that sense, the literature review motivates not just a better capability framework, but also the need for a surrounding system that can verify, parse, and execute plans against the robot's actual skill inventory.

### FSM semantics and the case for Fabric

The GPSFSM paper sharpens that argument by comparing Behaviour Trees and Finite State Machines more directly in the context of LLM-based generation. BTs benefit from an established XML representation and strong adoption in robotics, especially through frameworks such as BehaviorTree.CPP and Nav2. Their modularity and verification affordances make them attractive execution substrates. However, the GPSFSM paper argues that BTs still impose semantic overhead on a language model because the model must reason about hierarchical traversal, ticking semantics, and one-parent tree constraints in addition to the task itself.

FSMs provide a different trade-off. They represent behaviours as states with explicit event-triggered transitions, which the GPSFSM paper argues is conceptually closer to natural-language descriptions of actions and outcomes. Existing robotics FSM frameworks such as FlexBe, YASMIN, and SMACC2 demonstrate that FSMs are effective in practice, but they typically define states and transitions directly in code rather than through an intermediate textual representation. That limits their direct use in generative planning because the LLM cannot easily emit a portable, inspectable state-machine description \cite{zutell_ros_2022, gonzalez-santamarta_yasmin_2023}.

Fabric is motivated as a response to that gap. Rather than asking an LLM to generate executable source code or a full BT, the GPSFSM paper proposes a partially specified FSM representation that is textual, compositional, and grounded in Capabilities2 runners. This preserves the benefits of explicit behaviour structure while reducing reliance on ad hoc behaviour descriptions embedded directly in prompts. It also reframes PromptTools as more than a generic LLM connector: it becomes the mechanism by which capability descriptions, world information, and task statements are progressively assembled into prompts that remain compatible with the robot's underlying execution substrate.
